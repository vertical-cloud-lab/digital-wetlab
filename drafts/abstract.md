# Abstract

Sulfide mineral scales form when petroleum-derived H₂S—generated by thermochemical and bacterial sulfate reduction of crude oil hydrocarbons—reacts with dissolved Fe²⁺ and Pb²⁺ in produced waters, depositing iron sulfide and lead sulfide that restrict flow and foul equipment. These produced waters also contain petroleum-derived organic acids (acetic, propionic) from thermal maturation of kerogen, which modify sulfide precipitation through pH buffering and metal complexation. The Pitzer ion-interaction model predicts ion activities in concentrated electrolytes, yet parameterization for these interactions remains incomplete. Binary parameters (β⁰, β¹, Cᶲ) for Fe²⁺–HS⁻ and Pb²⁺–HS⁻ are absent from standard databases, and Pitzer parameters for metal–organic acid interactions (Fe²⁺–acetate, Pb²⁺–acetate, Fe²⁺–propionate, Pb²⁺–propionate) have never been determined—~48 unmeasured parameters that prevent rigorous prediction of sulfide-mineral solubility in produced-water brines.
This project determines these parameters through systematic high-throughput experiments. Using an Opentrons OT-2 platform, we map FeS and PbS precipitation boundaries across ~30,000 experiments spanning metal-ion, sulfide, and organic acid concentrations, ionic strength (0.5–5 molal), pH (4–9), and temperature (25–90 °C). Organic acids are introduced as sodium salts to independently vary concentration. Latin hypercube sampling provides broad coverage; a staged active learning strategy adaptively targets informative conditions near precipitation boundaries. Results are compared to PHREEQC Pitzer-model predictions; where deviations exceed ±0.2 saturation-index units, new parameters are regressed via nonlinear least-squares fitting and validated through cross-validation. The resulting framework fills a critical gap in electrolyte thermodynamics, delivering validated parameters for petroleum-industry scale prediction tools and enabling accurate prediction of how petroleum-derived compounds govern sulfide-scale formation in complex brines.
