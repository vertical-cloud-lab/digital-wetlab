Question: Have Pitzer parameters been regressed successfully from saturation-index (log Q/Ksp) or precipitation-boundary data, and what pitfalls are known compared with fitting osmotic or activity coefficients of single-salt solutions?

## Conclusion

**Yes—Pitzer parameters have been regressed successfully from measured solid–liquid equilibria.** An equilibrated saturation boundary supplies the condition \(\log_{10}(Q/K_{sp})=0\), so minimizing that residual is a thermodynamically sensible inverse-fitting strategy. The clearest example found here, however, fitted **solubility concentrations**, not an explicitly reported numerical log-saturation-index objective. A visible *precipitation onset* is a different, potentially kinetic observation and should not automatically be assigned zero saturation index. (zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 4-5, rard2007isopiesticdeterminationof pages 19-23, seepma2021controllingcaco3particle pages 1-2)

### Documented regression

Zhang and colleagues fitted \(\beta^{(0)}\), \(\beta^{(1)}\), and \(C^\phi\) for aqueous Na₂WO₄ **simultaneously with** the solubility product of Na₂WO₄·2H₂O, using ten measured saturation compositions along the Na₂WO₄–Na₂SO₄–H₂O boundary at 293.15 K. They reported a 2.484% average absolute relative deviation in tungstate molality. Their separate pure-water check gave 2.3579 mol kg⁻¹ calculated versus 2.4812 mol kg⁻¹ measured—useful agreement, but not an exact transfer. Their published table and plot show the fitted parameters and the measured-versus-calculated boundary. (zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 4-5, zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 5-6, zhang2016modelingfortungstic media 8aef6424, zhang2016modelingfortungstic media ae42aab5, zhang2016modelingfortungstic media b9b259b0)

Pitzer also noted that common-ion salt solubilities can provide the **best estimates for some mixed-electrolyte interaction terms**, while pure-salt binary parameters are ordinarily evaluated from osmotic or activity-coefficient measurements. This supports boundary-based estimation as a legitimate complement, not a general replacement, for solution-property data. Conversely, a study that *predicts* phase boundaries with an existing Pitzer database is not evidence that its parameters were regressed from those boundaries. (pitzer1986theoreticalconsiderationsof pages 5-7, nie2026phasediagramsof pages 23-27)

### Why boundary fitting is more fragile

1. **The aqueous model and the solid standard state are entangled.** At saturation, one measures a composition satisfying an activity product equal to \(K_{sp}\); one does not separately measure all ionic activity coefficients. If both \(K_{sp}\) and several Pitzer coefficients are free, their effects can compensate. Likewise, a wrong solid, hydrate, or solid Gibbs energy can be absorbed into apparently satisfactory aqueous parameters. Zhang et al. explicitly checked the competing Na₂SO₄ solids; for a hydrated dissolving solid, water activity also enters the equilibrium expression, as illustrated by the independently evaluated Li₂SO₄·H₂O solubility product. (zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 5-6, sinquefield1991amicrocomputersoftware pages 39-44, rard2007isopiesticdeterminationof pages 19-23)

2. **A boundary is a narrow thermodynamic constraint, not an activity curve.** One saturated composition supplies one equilibrium-product equation. A series spanning different salt concentrations is informative, but a single boundary trajectory can leave coefficients strongly correlated; mixed-ion terms require mixtures with sufficiently varied common ions and composition ratios. Single-salt osmotic/activity measurements instead follow solution properties over a molality range, permitting a more direct fit of binary concentration dependence; pure-salt measurements cannot identify mixed-electrolyte \(\theta\) and \(\psi\) terms. Pitzer cautioned against estimating \(C\) without measurements above ionic strength about 2 mol kg⁻¹ and against extending an inadequate virial representation to arbitrarily concentrated solutions. The parameter-correlation conclusion is an *identifiability inference* from these equilibrium equations and data requirements, not a reported uncertainty estimate for Zhang’s fit. (pitzer1986theoreticalconsiderationsof pages 3-5, pitzer1986theoreticalconsiderationsof pages 5-7, zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 4-5)

3. **A good phase-behavior fit need not give good solution properties.** In the tungstate study, aqueous parameters previously inferred from liquid–liquid extraction data produced grossly unrealistic single-salt mean activity coefficients; replacing them with the solid–liquid-equilibrium fit gave substantially closer agreement with independent isopiestic data, though measured at a different temperature. The lesson applies in both directions: test solubility-derived coefficients against independently measured osmotic, mean-activity, or emf data rather than treating boundary agreement as sufficient validation. Rard and colleagues provide the contrasting workflow: fit Li₂SO₄ solution-property measurements first, check independent emf data, and only then use a selected saturation molality to evaluate the hydrate’s \(K_s\). (zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 1-2, zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 6-7, rard2007isopiesticdeterminationof pages 19-23)

4. **Speciation and experimental equilibrium matter.** An omitted ion pair, acid–base species, or polymer can shift the *free-ion* activities entering \(Q\), making an interaction coefficient compensate for a species-model error; tungstate polymerization degraded the model’s higher-concentration predictions, and vanadate solubility modeling identifies uncertain pairing/complexation and activity coefficients as major limitations. Even conventional saturation measurements disagree: reported Li₂SO₄ saturation molalities spanned 0.074 mol kg⁻¹, or 2.4%, and the authors flagged inadequate equilibration when saturating from below. A visually observed precipitation threshold is less secure still: CaCO₃ particle-appearance times vary with ion ratio even at a specified supersaturation. Thus, an onset may constrain nucleation or detection conditions rather than \(Q=K_{sp}\). (zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 7-8, manninen2023acloserlook pages 9-10, rard2007isopiesticdeterminationof pages 19-23, seepma2021controllingcaco3particle pages 1-2)

**Practical implication:** fit well-characterized *equilibrium* solubilities across independent solution compositions, fixing or separately constraining solid-phase thermodynamics and as many binary parameters as possible; use the correct speciation and hydrate/water-activity conventions; then validate against withheld solubilities **and** independent osmotic/activity measurements. If only precipitation/no-precipitation observations are available, treat them as uncertain or censored boundary information, not precise zero-log-saturation-index measurements. The retrieved studies demonstrate successful solubility-based regression, but do **not** establish that an explicitly reported regression of numerical \(\log(Q/K_{sp})\) residuals is uniquely identifying or superior to fitting single-salt solution-property curves. (zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 4-5, zhang2016modelingfortungstic pages 5-6, pitzer1986theoreticalconsiderationsof pages 5-7, rard2007isopiesticdeterminationof pages 19-23, seepma2021controllingcaco3particle pages 1-2)
