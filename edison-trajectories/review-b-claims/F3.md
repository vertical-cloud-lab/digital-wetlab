Question: What was the original rationale for how Pitzer ion-interaction model parameters should be obtained: binary parameters (beta0, beta1, Cphi) from single-salt solution data and mixing parameters (theta, psi) from two-salt common-ion mixtures, using as few adjustable parameters as possible? Which papers by Pitzer and coworkers set this out, and when are mixing terms omitted?

## Original rationale and papers

Pitzer’s aim was to predict the properties of multicomponent electrolytes with equations **“no more complex than necessary.”** The strategy was sequential: determine what pure-salt measurements can establish first, then introduce only those additional parameters that mixed-salt measurements show to be consequential. This mattered because measurements existed for many pure electrolytes but only a small fraction of possible mixtures. (pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 1-7, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes.b pages 1-7)

| **II — Pitzer & Mayorga (1973)** | Obtained β⁽⁰⁾, β⁽¹⁾, and normally Cᵠ from activity- or osmotic-coefficient data for each pure salt. (pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes.b pages 1-7, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes.b pages 7-11) | Omit Cᵠ when measurements do not extend sufficiently into the high-molality range to determine it reliably. |
| **III — Pitzer & Mayorga (1974)** | Added β⁽²⁾ specifically for 2:2 electrolytes to represent their low-concentration behavior without requiring an explicit ion-pair species. (pitzer1974thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 1-7, pitzer1974thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 11-17) | Do not add β⁽²⁾ routinely to ordinary 1:1 or 2:1 salts; other weakly constrained terms were likewise omitted. |
| **IV — Pitzer & Kim (1974)** | Derived θ for unlike same-sign pairs (cation–cation or anion–anion) and ψ for two such ions plus an opposite-sign ion from residuals of two-salt common-ion mixture data after fixing the pure-salt parameters. The original sequence is **Pitzer, “Thermodynamics of Electrolytes. I. Theoretical Basis and General Equations,” *Journal of Physical Chemistry* 77, 268–277 (1973); Pitzer and Mayorga, Part II, “Activity and Osmotic Coefficients for Strong Electrolytes with One or Both Ions Univalent,” *Journal of Physical Chemistry* 77, 2300–2308 (1973); Pitzer and Mayorga, Part III, “Activity and Osmotic Coefficients for 2–2 Electrolytes,” *Journal of Solution Chemistry* 3, 539–546 (1974); and Pitzer and Kim, Part IV, “Activity and Osmotic Coefficients for Mixed Electrolytes,” *Journal of the American Chemical Society* 96, beginning at 5701 (1974).** Part IV was already circulated as a March 1974 preprint; a 1993 reprint should not be mistaken for its original publication date. (simoes2016estimationofthe pages 40-46, pitzer1974thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 11-17, silvester2011thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 9-11, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 1-7)

In **Part II**, β⁽⁰⁾ and β⁽¹⁾ characterize the cation–anion second-virial term, while Cᵠ characterizes a third-virial contribution. They were fitted to each *single-salt* solution’s osmotic- or activity-coefficient data, not freely refitted to every mixture. The authors used three parameters when sufficiently concentrated measurements supported them, but omitted Cᵠ when its effect could not be determined reliably from dilute data. **Part III** added a β⁽²⁾ term for the special behavior of 2–2 salts. (pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes.b pages 1-7, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes.b pages 7-11, pitzer1974thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 11-17)

In **Part IV**, the first mixture calculation used the already determined pure-salt parameters alone. Residuals from *two-salt mixtures sharing an ion*—for example, NaCl–KCl—then identified θ for unlike ions of the **same sign** (Na⁺–K⁺) and, when supported, ψ for their interaction with the common opposite-sign ion (Na⁺–K⁺–Cl⁻). Thus θ and ψ are *mixing corrections*, not parameters obtainable from either pure salt separately. Pitzer and Kim compared fits with θ = ψ = 0 against fits including significant corrections, then tested more complex mixtures without further adjustment. Their Table I displays the common-ion fitting comparison. (pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 7-10, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 10-13, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 13-17, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 17-21, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. media a1319a2e)

**When are mixing terms omitted?** They may be set to zero for a pure-salt-based prediction, or left out when mixture data show them to be negligible or cannot determine them; terms involving ions present only in small amounts can also be omitted when their contribution is immaterial. This is a controlled approximation, **not** a rule that all θ and ψ vanish. Repulsion keeps like-signed ions apart, so their short-range *difference* interactions are often small; nevertheless, significant terms should be retained for accurate concentrated-mixture predictions, particularly for the OH⁻ and Cs⁺ combinations highlighted by Pitzer and Kim. Their 1974 treatment also neglected the ionic-strength derivative of θ for practical fits; that simplification should not be confused with declaring every mixing effect zero. In later formulations, unlike-charge same-sign pairs can additionally have a theoretically calculated, ionic-strength-dependent electrostatic mixing contribution distinct from fitted short-range θ. (pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 7-10, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 13-17, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 21-26, pitzer1993thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 26-32, pitzer1986theoreticalconsiderationsof pages 5-7)
