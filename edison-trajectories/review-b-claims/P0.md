Question: In NaCl brines, how much do acetate and other short-chain carboxylates change Fe(II) and Pb(II) speciation and the solubility of FeS and PbS? Is this effect usually modeled with aqueous complex-formation constants (for example FeOAc+, PbOAc+, Pb(OAc)2 aq) rather than with ion-interaction activity coefficients?

## Acetate effects in NaCl brines

**Acetate can appreciably change Fe(II) and Pb(II) speciation, but its effect is conditional—not a fixed multiplier for FeS or PbS solubility.** A published model of an ore brine at 100 °C, pH 6, **2.0 molal total chloride** and **0.1 molal total acetate** assigns **16% of dissolved Fe** and **5.2% of dissolved Pb** to acetate complexes. Chloride complexes account for **47% of Fe** and **86% of Pb**. These are modeled fractions of dissolved metal, *not* measured changes in sulfide solubility. (giordano1994organicliganddistribution media 26ad65c1, giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 19-20)

The following comparison places that brine calculation alongside the underlying complexation constants and a second brine example. (giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 16-17, giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 17-19, giordano1994organicliganddistribution media 26ad65c1)

At 25 °C, compiled infinite-dilution cumulative formation constants are log β₁ = **0.87** for FeOAc⁺ and **2.25** for PbOAc⁺, and log β₂ = **1.89** for Fe(OAc)₂(aq) and **3.50** for Pb(OAc)₂(aq). The stronger intrinsic Pb–acetate association does not imply that acetate dominates Pb in a chloride-rich brine: chloride, pH, and competition for acetate determine the *actual* species fractions. The compilation treats propionate as having approximately similar complex stability to acetate and combines their concentrations in its models because high-temperature propionate data are lacking; it does not establish comparable quantitative Fe/Pb effects for every other short-chain carboxylate. (giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 16-17, giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 17-19, giordano1994organicliganddistribution media 26ad65c1)

## What can be said about FeS and PbS solubility?

At fixed temperature, pH, sulfide activity, salinity and mineral phase, binding dissolved Fe²⁺ or Pb²⁺ into acetate complexes lowers its free-ion fraction and **tends to raise the total dissolved metal compatible with sulfide saturation**. A simplified acetate-only expression for that tendency is a factor proportional to *1 + β₁a*OAc⁻ *+ β₂a*OAc⁻²; a brine calculation must also include chloride, sulfide and other aqueous complexes. The model species fractions above therefore demonstrate a potentially meaningful complexation contribution, but cannot be converted into a measured FeS- or PbS-solubility increase without a matched calculation that holds the other conditions fixed. (giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 16-17, giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 19-20, giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 17-19)

**No defensible universal “acetate increases FeS by X-fold and PbS by Y-fold” follows from the located studies.** The often-cited lead-acetate experiment measured dissolution of **anglesite (PbSO₄), not galena (PbS)**, in acetic-acid/sodium-acetate solutions rather than a specified NaCl brine. It yielded Pb-acetate formation constants and showed that anglesite solubility rose sharply above approximately 0.01 molal free acetate; its dissolution magnitude must not be attributed to PbS. For PbS, a separate acetate-leaching study involved oxidation and precipitation of secondary anglesite, not an isolated equilibrium acetate effect in NaCl brine. The reviewed brine models likewise do not provide a direct acetate-only equilibrium-solubility measurement for FeS or PbS. (giordano1989anglesite(pbso4)solubility pages 1-2, giordano1989anglesite(pbso4)solubility pages 2-3, nikkhou2021formationmechanismsof pages 184-188, giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 19-20)

The qualification matters particularly for PbS: a model of ore fluids found substantial Pb/Zn transport by carboxylate complexes plausible chiefly when reduced sulfur was very low; above **10⁻⁵ molal reduced sulfur**, substantial transport as those complexes was not predicted. Changing acetic acid also changes buffering and pH, so observed dissolution cannot automatically be assigned to metal–acetate binding. (giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 19-20, giordano1994organicliganddistribution pages 17-19)

## Complex formation versus activity coefficients

**Yes—specific metal–carboxylate association is ordinarily represented by explicit aqueous species and formation constants**, for example Fe²⁺ + OAc⁻ ⇌ FeOAc⁺ and Pb²⁺ + 2OAc⁻ ⇌ Pb(OAc)₂(aq). **But this is not an alternative to activity-coefficient modeling.** NaCl-rich fluids also need nonideality corrections—Pitzer or a comparable ion-interaction treatment—to convert concentrations to activities and predict equilibrium consistently. Giordano’s lead-acetate analysis explicitly used PbOAc⁺/Pb(OAc)₂ formation constants *and* Pitzer-based electrolyte activity coefficients; brine-geochemistry treatments likewise call for both aqueous-species data and an appropriate high-salinity activity model. Ion-interaction coefficients alone should not be interpreted as a substitute for an independently established, chemically identifiable metal–acetate complex. (giordano1989anglesite(pbso4)solubility pages 3-4, giordano1989anglesite(pbso4)solubility pages 4-5, kharakaUnknownyearusgeologicalsurvey pages 32-33)
