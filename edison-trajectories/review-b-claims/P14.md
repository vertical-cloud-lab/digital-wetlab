---

Question: In thermodynamic solubility studies of metal sulfides or carbonates in brines, are pH buffers (Good's buffers such as MES and HEPES, or bicarbonate) used or avoided, and do these buffers complex Pb(II) or Fe(II)? Is pH treated as a controlled variable or as a measured quantity set by composition and gas partial pressure?

## Answer

**There is no universal prohibition on pH buffers, but an added buffer changes the chemical system whose solubility is being measured.** In the brine-solubility examples examined, investigators generally specified the salts, carbonate inventory and/or gas conditions rather than adding MES or HEPES to hold pH constant. Kim and colleagues studied siderite in anoxic, high-salinity NaCl–Na₂CO₃ solutions without reporting an organic pH buffer; the solution pH decreased during aging as siderite partly converted to chukanovite. Angel and colleagues measured pH during Pb-solubility tests in natural and artificial seawater: it stayed at 8.15 ± 0.05 without an added organic buffer, except that the highest-Pb treatments needed a little NaOH to prevent acidification. These examples support **avoiding an unmodeled buffer**, not a rule that pH must never be adjusted. (kim2017solubilitymodelfor pages 22-24, kim2017solubilitymodelfor pages 26-29, angel2016leadsolubilityin pages 2-3, kim2017solubilitymodelfor media 9925f97c)

**Bicarbonate is not an inert substitute for a Good’s buffer in a carbonate-solubility experiment.** Adding it changes dissolved inorganic carbon and carbonate activity, as well as acid–base capacity. Fe(II) speciation models explicitly include FeHCO₃⁺, FeCO₃(aq) and Fe(CO₃)₂²⁻; seawater Pb speciation includes carbonato and mixed chloro-carbonato complexes. Carbonate therefore needs to be specified and included in the equilibrium calculation whether introduced as bicarbonate, carbonate, or dissolved CO₂. Kim’s observed mineral transformation in NaCl–Na₂CO₃ solutions illustrates that it can also affect the *solid phase* controlling solubility. (king1998roleofcarbonate pages 2-3, kim2017solubilitymodelfor pages 13-17, angel2016leadsolubilityin pages 1-2, kim2017solubilitymodelfor pages 26-29, kim2017solubilitymodelfor media 9925f97c)

**MES and HEPES are often treated as relatively weakly metal-complexing, not proven universally noncomplexing.** In Fe(II)-oxidation experiments—*not* mineral-solubility determinations—King treated HEPES and PIPES as operationally noncomplexing near pH 7.7: oxidation rates did not depend on their concentrations, unlike the marked dependence on carbonate concentration. A separate Fe(II)–magnetite study deliberately used MES and HEPES because of their expected low metal-complexing capacity. These findings do not establish zero binding in concentrated brine. For Pb(II), HEPES changed algal Pb uptake, but that observation does **not** itself demonstrate a Pb–HEPES complex; surface and biological effects were also implicated. The retrieved evidence provides no defensible Pb(II)–MES/HEPES or Fe(II)–MES/HEPES stability constant. In particular, a tabulated Pb(II) log β₁ of 4.32 belongs to **Bis-Tris**, not HEPES or MES. (king1998roleofcarbonate pages 3-4, peng2019redistributionofelectron pages 7-13, vasconcelos2002influenceofn‐2‐hydroxyethylpiperazine‐n′‐2‐ethanesulfonic pages 1-2, magyar2003spectropotentiometricanalysisof pages 2-4)

**pH is both a possible experimental target and an essential equilibrium observation; it is not automatically an independently fixed variable.** Investigators can choose an initial pH with acid/base, or impose a CO₂ gas mixture or a defined bicarbonate concentration. The resulting pH can then change as gas dissolves and minerals react, so the equilibrium value must be measured or, where direct measurement is impracticable, reconstructed from the specified composition and gas conditions. King describes carbonate experiments whose pH was adjusted by CO₂/N₂/O₂ bubbling; Angel measured and, only where needed, corrected pH; Kim reported a pH decrease but the examined passage does not document a measured *final* pH, instead calculating H⁺ activity from experimental constraints. At elevated pressure, pH measured after depressurization may differ from in-situ pH. Thus a reported **target**, a **measured equilibrium pH**, and a **model-calculated in-situ pH** should not be conflated. (king1998roleofcarbonate pages 2-3, angel2016leadsolubilityin pages 2-3, kim2017solubilitymodelfor pages 26-29, kaszuba2013experimentalperspectivesof pages 11-13)

**Scope:** The directly examined brine-solubility protocols here concern carbonates and seawater Pb, not a representative set of metal-*sulfide* solubility protocols. The same need to specify ligands and gas conditions applies to interpreting sulfide equilibria, but a categorical claim that sulfide-brine studies always avoid Good’s buffers is not established by these sources. (kim2017solubilitymodelfor pages 22-24, angel2016leadsolubilityin pages 2-3, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 36-37)
