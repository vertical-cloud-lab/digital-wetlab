Question: What explanations have been given for disagreement between modeled and measured solubilities of mackinawite or galena in brines, such as uncertainty in the second dissociation constant of H2S, uncontrolled H2S partial pressure, inaccurate bisulfide complex constants, or experimental error?

Disagreement has been attributed to **uncertain sulfide chemistry, imperfect control of solution conditions, and possible measurement artifacts**. These are distinct explanations, not a single established cause for both minerals.

- **H₂S dissociation—particularly for mackinawite.** Published values for the second dissociation constant, *K*ₐ₂ (HS⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + S²⁻), span approximately 10⁻¹² to 10⁻¹⁹ at room temperature. Sun and colleagues attributed much of that spread to difficulty measuring HS⁻ and S²⁻ and advised against using *K*ₐ₂-dependent, S²⁻-based expressions to predict mackinawite solubility. They favored an H⁺/HS⁻ expression instead. This explains why **alternative model formulations** can disagree; it does not establish that *K*ₐ₂ caused every model–experiment mismatch. (sun2008equilibriumexpressionsrelated pages 3-4, sun2008equilibriumexpressionsrelated pages 2-3)
- **H₂S partial pressure and pH.** Calculated iron-sulfide solubilities depend on H₂S pressure and pH; for nonstoichiometric mackinawite, redox conditions matter as well. An experiment with poorly constrained gas pressure or pH therefore need not match a calculation made at specified values. This is a *potential* explanation, not a demonstrated defect in every brine experiment. In particular, Bourcier and Barnes measured pressures before and after adding H₂S and CO₂ and used them to estimate gas fugacities for their galena comparison. (macdonald1992criticalissuesin pages 6-7, bourcier1987oresolutionchemistry; pages 13-14)
- **Bisulfide complexes and their constants.** Macdonald reported that adding Fe(HS)₂ to an iron-sulfide calculation produced implausibly high solubilities, pointing to a possibly incorrect assigned Gibbs energy for the dissolved species. More generally, Rickard and Luther cautioned that fitting solubility data to assumed metal–bisulfide complexes may give non-unique stability constants unless complex stoichiometry is independently established. Bourcier and Barnes used published Pb–bisulfide constants to calculate galena solubility, but **did not conclude** that those constants were wrong. (macdonald1992criticalissuesin pages 7-9, rickard2006metalsulfidecomplexes pages 20-22, bourcier1987oresolutionchemistry; pages 13-14, bourcier1987oresolutionchemistry; pages 14-16)
- **Experimental error or failure to measure equilibrium.** In hydrothermal galena experiments, Hemley and colleagues identified spalled galena fines that could dissolve during acid treatment and spuriously raise measured Pb; they excluded affected runs. They also reported PbS precipitation in sampling tubing and rapid initial galena dissolution followed by reprecipitation as the mixed-mineral system equilibrated. Such effects can shift a measured concentration without implying that the equilibrium model is necessarily wrong. (hemley1992hydrothermaloreformingprocesses pages 8-9, hemley1992hydrothermaloreformingprocesses pages 5-8)

The size of a disagreement matters. In Bourcier and Barnes’s **5 m NaCl–0.5 m CaCl₂ brine**, measured and calculated galena solubilities across 100–355 °C were **within one log unit**, which the authors described as comparable to their zinc-solubility agreement. Their results therefore support uncertainty in the comparison, but not a claim that uncontrolled H₂S pressure, faulty Pb–bisulfide constants, or experimental error was proven to be its cause. (bourcier1987oresolutionchemistry; pages 13-14, bourcier1987oresolutionchemistry; pages 14-16)
