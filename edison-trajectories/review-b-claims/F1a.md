Question: What solid phases form when dissolved Fe(II) and sulfide are mixed in NaCl solutions at pH 4-9 and 25-90 C (for example nanocrystalline or disordered mackinawite, crystalline mackinawite, greigite, pyrrhotite, Fe(OH)2, or siderite when bicarbonate is present)? How does the identity of the first-formed solid depend on time, temperature and supersaturation, and how have studies identified the phase (XRD, Raman, Mossbauer)?

## Phase selection in Fe(II)–sulfide solutions

**The best-supported first solid formed by mixing dissolved Fe(II) and sulfide under anoxic, supersaturated aqueous conditions is nanocrystalline, disordered tetragonal FeS—mackinawite—not greigite or pyrrhotite.** Older reports called the poorly diffracting precipitate “amorphous FeS,” but diffraction and nanoscale structural measurements show mackinawite-like order. This conclusion is strongest for the studied ambient-temperature mixtures; the available studies do **not** establish one universal first phase for every NaCl concentration, pH 4–9, and temperature from 25 to 90 °C. In particular, a phase found after hours on corroding steel cannot automatically be assigned as the first homogeneous precipitate from mixed solutions. (rickard2006thecompositionof pages 1-2, wolthers2003thestructureof pages 1-3, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 10-11)

### Time, temperature, and supersaturation

**Minutes to a day.** In oxygen-excluding Fe(II)–sulfide preparations, the initial precipitate is composed of very small, poorly ordered FeS domains. Wolthers and colleagues resolved two *mackinawite-like structural components*, MkA and MkB, rather than demonstrating two unrelated minerals. The relative MkA/MkB contributions to the XRD (001) peak changed from 22/78% in material collected within 0.5 h to 4/96% after approximately 2 h in suspension and 2/98% after one day: the less-ordered component diminished rapidly. Those figures are **relative peak areas**, not measured bulk mass fractions. Direct reaction of dissolved Fe(II) with sulfide tends to yield less crystalline mackinawite than reaction of sulfide with metallic iron; consequently, sharp reflections from the latter should not be taken as the expected immediate signature of the former. (wolthers2003thestructureof pages 1-3, wolthers2003thestructureof pages 6-7, wolthers2003thestructureof media 880cdbf9)

**Supersaturation controls whether and how rapidly a solid appears, not a demonstrated switch to primary pyrrhotite.** A recent synthesis of nucleation evidence estimates that, at standard conditions, mackinawite can become detectable below approximately tenfold supersaturation; a model limiting-rate estimate is about 1.08-fold. Once supersaturated at low-millimolar Fe(II) and sulfide concentrations, calculated nucleation rates increase steeply. These are room-temperature observations and model estimates, **not measured phase boundaries throughout 25–90 °C or for all NaCl activities**. Because the ion activity product, rather than uncorrected analytical concentrations, defines supersaturation, pH-dependent sulfide speciation and salinity matter when comparing recipes. At the acidic end of the range, mackinawite becomes more soluble below roughly pH 6, so sufficient supersaturation must be established before predicting a persistent solid. (rickard2026thechemistryof pages 14-15, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 25-26, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 18-19)

**Warmer or longer-aged systems can acquire additional phases, but the reactants and substrate matter.** A reported mackinawite preparation was still principally mackinawite, with trace greigite, after three weeks at 85 °C. Conversely, XRD detected pyrrhotite after 168 h on steel exposed at 80 °C to 3.5 wt% NaCl and high-H₂S/CO₂ gas mixtures; lower-H₂S tests identified mackinawite. That experiment involves steel dissolution, interfacial reactions and surface precipitation, and therefore does not demonstrate *pyrrhotite-first* nucleation from dissolved Fe(II) plus sulfide at 80–90 °C. A review of thermal experiments reports transformation of **wet pre-existing mackinawite** to hexagonal pyrrhotite after 12 h at 150 °C, underscoring why simply heating a newly mixed solution to 90 °C does not justify assigning pyrrhotite without phase measurements. (rickard2026thechemistryof pages 14-15, wang2016ironsulfideand pages 12-14, pessu2018astudyof pages 5-6, pessu2018astudyof pages 6-7, pessu2018astudyof media cac08816)

**Greigite is a conditional secondary product.** It contains Fe(III), whereas newly precipitated Fe(II) monosulfide contains Fe(II); its appearance therefore involves a redox or disproportionation pathway, not just a change of FeS crystal habit. In anaerobic experiments that *began with nanocrystalline mackinawite* and added polysulfide, in-situ diffraction followed greigite as an intermediate on the way to pyrite at **100–200 °C**, outside the specified temperature window. Greigite can also arise during oxidation or sample handling, so its detection does not, by itself, prove that it was the first solid when the solutions were combined. (hunger2007greigiteatrue pages 2-3, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 23-24, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 10-11)

**Hydroxide and carbonate are competing or precursor solids, not obligatory Fe(II)–sulfide products.** Fe(OH)₂ requires sufficient hydroxide activity, and siderite (FeCO₃) requires carbonate as well as favorable saturation. In an anoxic *steel* experiment with bicarbonate/carbonate but initially **without sulfide**, investigators reported a thin Fe(OH)₂/Fe₃O₄ film and cubic siderite crystals. Subsequent sulfide exposure produced mackinawite and replaced the Fe(III)-bearing oxide; whether siderite itself converted was **not determined**. This demonstrates why bicarbonate can permit siderite, but does not establish that Fe(OH)₂ or siderite precedes FeS in every simultaneous Fe(II)–sulfide–bicarbonate mixture. (goldman2019electrochemicalinvestigationintoa pages 138-143, goldman2019electrochemicalinvestigationinto pages 138-143)

### How the phases were identified

* **XRD and local structure:** Conventional powder XRD of freshly precipitated FeS can show only a broad basal feature near **5 Å** or broad mackinawite reflections. For example, a controlled synthesis produced broad peaks near **5.05, 2.97 and 2.31 Å**, among others, consistent with nanocrystalline mackinawite. Low-angle XRD, electron diffraction/HRTEM and high-energy-XRD pair-distribution analysis reveal nanoscale tetragonal order that sharp-peak XRD alone may miss. With ordering and growth, mackinawite reflections become more distinct. In-situ diffraction can instead follow the appearance of *new, phase-specific* greigite or pyrite reflections. (rickard2006thecompositionof pages 1-2, hunger2007greigiteatrue pages 2-3, wolthers2003thestructureof pages 3-4, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 20-21, rickard1997kineticsofpyrite pages 2-3)
* **Raman:** Surface Raman measurements can track sulfide films over time, but overlapping or weak bands and oxidation make a uniquely named phase assignment hazardous. In the bicarbonate/steel study, spectra after longer exposure were interpreted as consistent with progression from mackinawite toward greigite and pyrite, while the authors explicitly judged them **insufficiently defined to confirm each individual intermediate**. Raman evidence for an altered surface should therefore be checked against diffraction and air-protected controls, rather than used alone to declare the first bulk precipitate. (rickard2026thechemistryof pages 23-24, goldman2019electrochemicalinvestigationinto pages 138-143)
* **⁵⁷Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy:** Mössbauer probes Fe’s local electronic and magnetic environment even when Bragg diffraction is weak, but **a single line, doublet or sextet is not a unique mineral name**. In one comparative study, freshly mixed homogeneous Fe(II)–sulfide gave a low-temperature single line; after one month of dry anoxic aging a six-line component also appeared. The investigators cautioned that the spectra could reflect different FeS structures, defects or particle histories, and considered *cubic FeS* as a possible assignment for the fresh material. Thus a Mössbauer-only assignment of every fresh FeS solid to crystalline mackinawite—or every magnetic component to greigite—would overstate the evidence. Combine it with XRD and, where possible, microscopy while maintaining anoxic preparation and measurement. (wan2015interactionbetweenferric pages 180-184, wan2015interactionbetweenferric pages 171-176)

**Bottom line:** For a newly mixed, oxygen-free, sufficiently supersaturated dissolved-Fe(II)/sulfide solution, report the first identifiable solid as **disordered/nanocrystalline mackinawite-like FeS**, specifying the observation time and structural method. Report ordered mackinawite, greigite, pyrrhotite, Fe(OH)₂ or siderite as first-formed *only where that particular solution experiment resolves their order of appearance*. The accessible evidence supports later ordering and condition-dependent secondary products, but not a general pH–temperature–NaCl phase map proving that one of those alternatives nucleates first throughout 25–90 °C. (wolthers2003thestructureof pages 1-3, wolthers2003thestructureof pages 6-7, pessu2018astudyof pages 5-6, goldman2019electrochemicalinvestigationintoa pages 138-143, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 14-15)
