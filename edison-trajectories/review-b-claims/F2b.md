Question: How does the apparent solubility of freshly precipitated FeS change as disordered or nanocrystalline mackinawite ages or transforms, and what solubility constants have been reported for amorphous FeS versus crystalline mackinawite, troilite and pyrrhotite?

## Aging and apparent solubility

Freshly precipitated FeS is initially the **most soluble** of the iron monosulfide forms considered here. As it ages, its measured solubility decreases and can approach that of crystalline mackinawite. Davison’s comparison of fresh and progressively aged precipitates documents this direction of change, but does not establish a universal time-dependent rate or a single constant for every preparation. (davison1991thesolubilityof pages 6-8)

The traditional name **“amorphous FeS”** is partly operational: material that gives little or no conventional X-ray diffraction pattern can be *nanocrystalline, disordered mackinawite*, rather than a distinct structureless phase. Wolthers and colleagues found a mixture of small, less-ordered and larger mackinawite domains; the proportion of the small domains declines with age, and more crystalline mackinawite develops over days to months. Their diffraction patterns show structural change from a fresh precipitate through approximately one day. Preparation conditions and particle populations therefore affect *apparent* solubility, so a fresh precipitate need not have a unique equilibrium constant. Further transformation may produce greigite and, under appropriate conditions, other iron sulfides; that is a change of controlling solid, not merely ripening of unchanged mackinawite. (wolthers2003thestructureof pages 1-3, wolthers2003thestructureof media 49b11d56, wolthers2003thestructureof pages 9-10)

## Comparable reported constants

Davison’s 25 °C (298 K) compilation defines the activity-based dissolution product as **Ksp = a(Fe²⁺)a(HS⁻)/a(H⁺)**, with hydrogen ion represented using the NBS pH scale, and **pKsp = −log₁₀ Ksp**. Thus these numbers describe FeS(s) + H⁺ ⇌ Fe²⁺ + HS⁻; they are *not* values of a(Fe²⁺)a(S²⁻). At the same pH and sulfide activity, a lower pKsp corresponds to a higher dissolved Fe²⁺ activity. (davison1991thesolubilityof pages 1-3, davison1991thesolubilityof pages 6-8)

| FeS phase | pKsp at 25 °C | log10 Ksp (sign reversal) | Relative apparent solubility at fixed pH and sulfide activity |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Freshly precipitated “amorphous” FeS | 2.95 ± 0.10 | −2.95 | Highest |
| Crystalline mackinawite | 3.60 ± 0.20 | −3.60 | Lower; K ≈ 4.5-fold below fresh FeS |
| Pyrrhotite | 5.10 ± 0.15 | −5.10 | Much lower; K ≈ 140-fold below fresh FeS |
| Troilite | 5.25 ± 0.20 | −5.25 | Lowest listed; K ≈ 200-fold below fresh FeS |


*Table: Davison’s activity-based constants use Ksp = a(Fe²⁺)a(HS⁻)/a(H⁺), not an S²⁻ convention; log10 Ksp is therefore the sign reversal of pKsp. At fixed pH and sulfide activity, a smaller pKsp denotes greater apparent solubility. (davison1991thesolubilityof pages 1-3, davison1991thesolubilityof pages 6-8)*

On this common convention, the fresh-to-mackinawite change is about **0.65 log unit**, or a **4.5-fold decrease in Ksp**. Troilite and pyrrhotite have substantially lower, nearly indistinguishable reported solubilities; their respective pKsp values exceed that of fresh FeS by 2.30 and 2.15 log units. These are phase comparisons under a specified reaction convention, **not** a claim that every aging precipitate passes through all four phases. (davison1991thesolubilityof pages 6-8)

**Convention matters:** A more recent mackinawite treatment also reports an *intrinsic*, approximately pH-independent dissolution to neutral FeS⁰(aq), **FeS(s) ⇌ FeS⁰(aq)**, with **log K ≈ −5.7 at 25 °C**. That value should not be substituted for the mackinawite entry above: its aqueous product and defining reaction differ from the Fe²⁺/HS⁻/H⁺ product. (rickard2026thechemistryof pages 12-13, rickard2026thechemistryof pages 13-14)
