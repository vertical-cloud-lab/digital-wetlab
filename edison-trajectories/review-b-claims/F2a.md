Question: Does the onset of FeS or PbS precipitation detected within minutes to hours of mixing correspond to equilibrium solubility? What do studies report on nucleation supersaturation thresholds, induction times, and the time needed to reach solubility equilibrium for iron and lead sulfides, and how have sulfide solubility studies shown equilibrium, for example by approaching it from both undersaturation and supersaturation?

## Precipitation onset is not a solubility measurement

**No.** Detecting FeS or PbS within minutes or hours shows that a detectable solid or aggregate has formed under those mixing conditions. It does not show that the remaining solution has attained the equilibrium solubility of an identified mineral. A solution can be supersaturated before nucleation becomes detectable; after nucleation, dissolved concentrations can continue changing as particles grow, aggregate, dissolve, or transform. Detection also depends on whether nanoparticles pass through the filter used to define “dissolved” metal. (davison1991thesolubilityof pages 16-18, lewis2010reviewofmetal pages 4-5, rickard2006thecompositionof pages 1-2)

### Nucleation thresholds and induction times

For **FeS**, a review of mackinawite kinetics reports precipitation on a roughly **1-millisecond-to-1-second** scale under the conditions discussed; this is a formation timescale, *not* a measured time to attain solubility equilibrium. In a separate stopped-flow experiment using 0.1 M ferrous chloride and 0.1 M sodium sulfide, an FeHS⁺ intermediate appeared within seconds and decomposed over the next few minutes. Thus, even where the first Fe–S reaction is extremely fast, the observed species can still evolve after mixing. (diener2012structuralincorporationof pages 47-52, lewis2010reviewofmetal pages 3-4)

For **PbS**, the reviewed precipitation work describes a sufficiently rapid reaction that nitrate could be trapped in the product. Experiments on metal–sulfide clusters, including Pb-containing clusters, found controllable cluster sizes below approximately **2 µM of each reactant** and substantial aggregation above **10 µM**. Those concentrations describe the particular cluster experiments—not a PbS saturation ratio, a universal nucleation threshold, or a measured induction time. The sources examined do **not** establish a defensible numerical FeS or PbS critical supersaturation or a general PbS induction period: induction depends on composition, mixing, surfaces, and the chosen detection method. A sometimes-quoted threshold of **greater than 10¹⁴ at pH 6.5** concerns *direct pyrite (FeS₂) nucleation*, **not FeS or PbS**, and must not be transferred to them. (lewis2010reviewofmetal pages 4-5, diener2012structuralincorporationof pages 52-56)

### How long does solubility equilibration take?

There is **no single transferable equilibration time** for iron and lead sulfides. The first FeS precipitate is particularly important to characterize: Davison’s assessment gives, at 298 K and using **K = a(Fe²⁺)a(HS⁻)/a(H⁺)**, **pK ≈ 2.95 ± 0.10** for freshly precipitated, operationally “amorphous” iron sulfide, versus **3.6 ± 0.2** for mackinawite. Ageing lowered the measured solubility of fresh material toward the mackinawite value. Subsequent structural work found that freshly precipitated nanoparticulate FeS can itself be tetragonal mackinawite despite weak diffraction, underscoring why phase, particle size, preparation, and ageing must be reported rather than assigning a crystalline-phase solubility to an early black precipitate by appearance alone. (davison1991thesolubilityof pages 1-3, davison1991thesolubilityof pages 6-8, rickard2006thecompositionof pages 1-2)

An explicit example of the longer times needed to *test* equilibrium comes from Hemley and colleagues’ **hydrothermal**, buffered assemblages containing Fe sulfides and galena (PbS): runs lasted **8–12 weeks at 300 °C, 3–4 weeks at 400 °C, 1–2 weeks at 500 °C, and about 5 days at 600 °C**. The authors state that long runs were needed both to raise initially undersaturated solutions to saturation and to bring initially oversaturated solutions *down* to it. These durations belong to a mixed-mineral, chloride-rich, high-temperature system; they are **not measured room-temperature equilibration times for FeS or PbS**. (hemley1992hydrothermaloreformingprocesses pages 4-5, hemley1992hydrothermaloreformingprocesses pages 5-8)

### What constitutes evidence of equilibrium?

A stronger solubility experiment maintains controlled pH, sulfide speciation, temperature, and oxygen exposure; identifies the solid; measures dissolved metal repeatedly until its concentration or ion-activity product stabilizes; and tests whether dissolution from **undersaturation** and precipitation from **supersaturation** converge on the same phase-specific result. Davison reports that Berner’s mackinawite measurements approached equilibrium from **both directions**, while Kolthoff and Griffith also made supersaturation-side measurements on freshly precipitated iron sulfide; their ageing experiments revealed why the material’s history matters. For greigite, Davison cautioned that an apparent disagreement between the two directions involved an electroneutrality problem in the undersaturation data—agreement should be checked, not assumed. (davison1991thesolubilityof pages 1-3, davison1991thesolubilityof pages 6-8, davison1991thesolubilityof pages 8-11)

For the Fe/Pb-bearing hydrothermal assemblage, Hemley and colleagues used successive equilibration runs to locate a solubility plateau, then replicate runs initiated near it, including tests from above and below, and an independent hot-fluid extraction method to address precipitation during cooling. **That convergence and reproducibility—not the first observation of FeS or PbS—are the relevant evidence for an operational solubility equilibrium.** (hemley1992hydrothermaloreformingprocesses pages 4-5, hemley1992hydrothermaloreformingprocesses pages 5-8, hemley1992hydrothermaloreformingprocesses media ea21b255)
