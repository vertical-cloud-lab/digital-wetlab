## Significance of Organic-Organic Anion Interaction Parameters in the Pitzer Model for Concentrated Brines

### 1. Importance of θ(acetate–propionate) and ψ(acetate–propionate–Na⁺) for Mineral Solubility Prediction

In the Pitzer ion-interaction framework, the like-charge mixing parameter θ and the ternary parameter ψ account for short-range interactions between ions of the same sign and their modification by a third ion. Pitzer and Kim (1974) demonstrated that these mixing/difference terms are "always small" and are "relatively most important for singly charged ions," becoming less important for higher-charge ions (pitzer1974thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 1-7). For inorganic anion pairs in concentrated brines, Kim and Frederick (1988) compiled θ values for 49 ternary mixtures and found the typical range for anion–anion θ parameters spans approximately −0.05 to +0.11 (kim1988evaluationofpitzer pages 5-6, kim1988evaluationofpitzer pages 4-5). Reference values include θ(Cl⁻–SO₄²⁻) = 0.02, θ(Cl⁻–OH⁻) = −0.050, and θ(Cl⁻–HCO₃⁻) = 0.0735 (davis2014productionofexpendable pages 110-117, kim1988evaluationofpitzer pages 5-6).

For an acetate–propionate pair specifically, no direct measurement of θ(acetate–propionate) or ψ(acetate–propionate–Na⁺) has been reported in the literature. Given that acetate and propionate are structurally similar monovalent carboxylate anions with comparable Pitzer single-electrolyte parameters (see below), one would expect their mutual mixing parameter to be small — likely near zero — by analogy with other chemically similar ion pairs. Even if θ(acetate–propionate) were of typical inorganic magnitude (~0.01–0.07), its contribution to activity coefficients and mineral solubility predictions would be negligible in most produced-water scenarios because it enters the Pitzer equations multiplied by the product of both organic acid molalities (m_acetate × m_propionate), which is extremely small in most applications (pitzer1974thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 7-10, oelkers2009thermodynamicdatabasesfor pages 23-25, clegg1992thermodynamicsofmulticomponent pages 1-2).

### 2. Organic Acid Concentrations Relative to Inorganic Anions in Produced Waters

A quantitative comparison of organic acid and inorganic ion concentrations in petroleum produced waters reveals that organic acids are typically 2–4 orders of magnitude lower than the dominant inorganic anions. In many produced waters, acetate ranges from less than 11 mg/L to approximately 30 mg/L, with propionate at lower levels still (~5–20 mg/L) (akob2015organicandinorganic pages 5-6, bostick2002characterizationofsoluble pages 37-39). By contrast, chloride concentrations in oilfield brines are typically 40,000–115,000 mg/L (bostick2002characterizationofsoluble pages 37-39). Total volatile fatty acids (VFAs) in Louisiana oil-field brines were measured at 2–166 mg/L, accounting for 5–35% of total titration alkalinity (hanor1986distributionofdissolved pages 1-2).

In exceptional cases — particularly in Cenozoic reservoirs at subsurface temperatures of 80–140°C — acetate concentrations can reach up to ~1,500 mg/L (Gulf of Thailand) or even up to ~10,000 mg/L in rare instances (kharaka1993generationofaliphatic pages 1-2, lundegard1994distributionandoccurrence pages 14-17). However, total organic acid anion concentrations exceeding ~3,000 mg/L (approximately 0.05 m acetate-equivalent) are described as rare, and truly molar concentrations (≈1 M) of organic acids are not reported in the formation water literature (lundegard1994distributionandoccurrence pages 1-4, lundegard1994distributionandoccurrence pages 4-7). Even at the high end (~0.05–0.17 m acetate), these concentrations remain far below the 1–5 molal chloride concentrations typical of concentrated brines.

### 3. Theoretical Scaling of Minor-Minor Ion Mixing Contributions

The Pitzer excess Gibbs energy expression for multicomponent solutions includes like-charge interaction terms that appear as products of molalities multiplied by interaction parameters. Specifically, anion–anion binary mixing terms enter as m_a × m_a' × Φ_aa', and ternary terms enter as m_a × m_a' × m_c × ψ_aa'c (oelkers2009thermodynamicdatabasesfor pages 23-25, davis2014productionofexpendable pages 37-41, rowland2015aqueouselectrolytesolution pages 1-2). When both organic acid anions are at millimolar concentrations (~10⁻³ m) while chloride is at molar concentrations (~1–5 m), the acetate–propionate mixing contribution scales as ~10⁻³ × 10⁻³ = 10⁻⁶, which is negligible compared to the Cl⁻–organic anion cross terms (~1 × 10⁻³ = 10⁻³) or the dominant Cl⁻ self-interaction terms (~1 × 1 = 1) (pitzer1974thermodynamicsofelectrolytes. pages 7-10, clegg1992thermodynamicsofmulticomponent pages 1-2).

May and Rowland (2017) further noted that while the Pitzer framework is in principle capable of representing trace-ion activity coefficients, practical application to multicomponent mixtures is "severely constrained" by the large number of required parameters that "cannot feasibly be determined experimentally" for all possible ion pairs (may2017thermodynamicmodelingof pages 10-13). Minor-minor interaction terms are thus both theoretically negligible (scaling with the product of small concentrations) and practically inaccessible (lacking experimental data for parameterization).

**Single-electrolyte parameters** are well-established: Das (2004) reports β⁽⁰⁾ = 0.14327, β⁽¹⁾ = 0.31509, C^φ = −0.00605 for sodium acetate and β⁽⁰⁾ = 0.17427, β⁽¹⁾ = 0.37265, C^φ = −0.01026 for sodium propionate (das2004pitzerioninteraction pages 6-11, das2004pitzerioninteraction media 8dceafd0). Similar parameters from Mizera et al. (1999) give β⁽⁰⁾ = 0.1426, β⁽¹⁾ = 0.2200, C^φ = −0.00629 for Na⁺–Ac⁻ (mizera1999dissociationconstantsof pages 9-12).

**Halide–carboxylate mixing parameters** have been determined: Hernández-Luis et al. (1993) measured Pitzer mixing parameters for the NaBr + NaAcO + H₂O and NaBr + NaPrO + H₂O systems, reporting θ values of approximately −0.022 for Br⁻–Ac⁻ and +0.009 for Br⁻–Pro⁻, along with corresponding ψ parameters (hernandez‐luis1993activitycoefficientsof pages 3-4). These magnitudes are consistent with typical anion–anion θ values for inorganic pairs.

**No measurements exist for θ(acetate–propionate) or ψ(acetate–propionate–cation).** A comprehensive literature search found no study that has determined these specific organic–organic anion mixing parameters. Mizera et al. (1999) explicitly stated that "all like ion and ternary Pitzer parameters were set to zero" when modeling carboxylic acids in NaCl media, acknowledging that such parameters might be needed for complex datasets but were unavailable (mizera1999dissociationconstantsof pages 9-12). Clegg and Seinfeld (2006) included some organic–organic interaction parameters for dicarboxylic acid systems in their Pitzer model but noted these were "uncertain," had "small" effects, and the parameterization was "not unique" due to limited data (clegg2006thermodynamicmodelsof pages 11-13).

### 5. Standard Practice in Produced-Water Geochemistry Modeling

The standard practice in oilfield scale prediction is to **exclude organic–organic anion Pitzer interaction parameters**. Several lines of evidence support this conclusion:

**Surrogate-species approach:** Kan et al. (2013), developers of the ScaleSoftPitzer (SSP) software, treat acetic acid as "a model species for all organic acids" and incorporate organic acids primarily through their effect on pH and alkalinity balance rather than through detailed organic–organic Pitzer parameterization (kan2013interrelationshipofco pages 8-10, kan2013interrelationshipofco pages 4-5, kan2013interrelationshipofco pages 1-2). The scale prediction model includes organic acid speciation (e.g., Ac⁻ in the charge balance) and uses empirical correction factors (f₁, f₂) to compensate for uncertainties in equilibrium constants and activity coefficients.

**Zero-parameter convention:** Mizera et al. (1999) set all like-ion and ternary Pitzer parameters to zero when modeling carboxylic acid dissociation in NaCl brines, retaining only the cation–organic anion binary parameters (e.g., Na⁺–Ac⁻) needed to represent single-electrolyte behavior (mizera1999dissociationconstantsof pages 9-12).

**Statistical insignificance of additional parameters:** Marshall et al. (1995) evaluated extended Pitzer models that include neutral organic species (RCOOH) and additional interaction constants but found that "simultaneous determination from a single data set is often statistically unjustified because the extra parameters do not significantly reduce residuals" (marshall1995leastsquaresanalysisof pages 9-10). At even 4 M sodium acetate, neutral species concentrations are predicted to be only millimolar, rendering their interaction terms negligible.

**Separate treatment of complex organics:** Turner et al. (2016), in their SCOR Working Group review for Pitzer-based seawater models, noted that natural organic matter falls outside the Pitzer framework and is typically handled by independent models (WHAM, SHM, NICA-Donnan) rather than by detailed organic–organic Pitzer interaction parameters (turner2016towardaqualitycontrolled pages 4-5).

The justification for excluding organic–organic parameters is threefold: (i) organic acid concentrations in most produced waters are too low relative to inorganic ions for organic–organic terms to contribute meaningfully; (ii) the Pitzer mixing terms scale with the product of both minor-ion molalities, making them inherently second-order small; and (iii) insufficient experimental data exist to constrain these parameters reliably. Instead, the primary importance of organic acids in scale prediction lies in their effect on pH (through acid-base equilibria and alkalinity balance) and their complexation with divalent cations, rather than in their direct contribution to activity coefficient calculations via organic–organic mixing terms (kan2013interrelationshipofco pages 8-10, kan2013interrelationshipofco pages 4-5).

### Summary

Organic–organic anion interaction parameters (θ and ψ) in the Pitzer model are negligible for predicting mineral solubility and scale precipitation in petroleum produced waters under virtually all practical conditions. This conclusion rests on the convergence of three factors: (1) the concentrations of organic acid anions are typically 2–4 orders of magnitude below those of the dominant inorganic anions; (2) the Pitzer mixing terms scale as the product of the minor-ion molalities, making organic–organic contributions at least six orders of magnitude smaller than dominant inorganic interactions; and (3) no experimental measurements of θ(acetate–propionate) or similar organic–organic pairs exist, and the standard modeling practice is to set these parameters to zero without appreciable loss of accuracy.
